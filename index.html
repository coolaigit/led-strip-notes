COB vs SMD LED Strip: What Actually Changes in a Commercial Install

Specifying LED strip looks simple until the first mock-up. Two products with the same wattage, the same CCT and the same claimed lumen output can behave completely differently on a ceiling, and the difference usually comes down to the emitter package rather than the datasheet headline.

Why the emitter type matters more than the wattage

SMD strip places discrete diodes on the board at intervals. COB strip mounts a continuous array of die directly on the substrate and covers it with a single phosphor layer. Electrically both are constant-current loads; optically they are not the same product. SMD gives you a series of bright points, COB gives you a line. Everything that follows — dotting, bend radius, heat spread, diffuser choice — follows from that one difference.

Where dotting shows up first

Dotting is not visible in a photo taken from two metres away. It appears when the strip is close to a reflective surface, when it runs along a cove with a shallow setback, or when the diffuser is thin. Three situations catch people out:

  • Aluminium channel with a 10–12 mm internal width and a low-profile PC cover: SMD at 60 LED/m reads as a dotted line, COB reads as a continuous one.
  • Backlit panels and signage where the strip sits 20–40 mm behind an acrylic face.
  • Tight bends around columns, where the bend radius forces a shorter pitch and the gap between diodes becomes visible.

If the design has any of these, the emitter choice is settled before the CCT is. If the strip is hidden behind a deep cove or a thick opal diffuser, SMD is usually the cheaper answer and there is no optical reason to pay for COB.

CRI and colour consistency

CRI above 90 matters in retail, hospitality and any application where skin tones or merchandise colour are being judged. The number to ask for is not just Ra but the R9 value, because saturated red is where cheap high-CRI products fall down. Equally important is binning: a 3-step MacAdam ellipse keeps adjacent runs from drifting apart when two reels from different batches meet in the same ceiling line. Ask for the bin code in writing, and ask whether the phosphor is applied per batch or per reel.

Voltage, run length and driver placement

Low-voltage strip (12V or 24V) is limited by voltage drop. A 24V COB run typically holds colour and brightness further than 12V before the far end dims, which matters on long perimeter runs. High-voltage strip (110V or 220V) solves the run-length problem and removes the remote driver, at the cost of a thicker board, stricter safety handling and less flexibility at corners.

  • 24V is the default for architectural runs above 5 m.
  • 12V is fine for short runs, vehicles and tight cabinets.
  • 110V/220V makes sense for long facade lines where a driver every 5 m is not practical.

Whatever the voltage, decide where the driver lives before the ceiling is closed. A driver in a sealed void with no ventilation is the most common cause of a two-year failure on an install that was specified for ten.

IP rating is not a number you pick last

IP54 covers dust and splashing; IP65 handles jet spray; IP67 survives temporary immersion. Outdoor soffits, bathroom zones and cold-room applications each need a different answer, and the sealing method matters as much as the number: silicone extrusion, heat-shrink end caps and glued connectors age differently. On outdoor work, specify the connector type and the end-sealing method explicitly, because that is where water actually gets in.

A procurement checklist that survives sample approval

  1. Ask for the LM-80 data and the L70 projection, not just the L70 number.
  2. Request the bin code and the CRI/R9 test report for the exact reel you will receive.
  3. Build a 5 m mock-up on the real channel and diffuser, at the real setback, and look at it at night.
  4. Measure the far-end brightness on the full run length, not on a 1 m sample.
  5. Confirm the driver derating at the ambient temperature inside the void, not at 25 °C on a bench.
  6. Keep one reel from the production batch as a reference before the ceiling closes.

None of this is exotic, but it is the difference between a strip that looks right on day one and one that still looks right after two summer cycles.
